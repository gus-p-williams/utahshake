# Activity card: One quake, two waves

A 10-minute predict-and-watch demo on the projector, for a physics waves unit or as the first
lesson before [Quake Locator](wave-race-card.md). The class predicts, then watches two cartoons:
[How P and S waves move rock](../app/wave-motion/index.html) and
[Quake Waves](../app/quake-waves/index.html) (students can read the
[Quake Waves guide](../apps/quake-waves.md) afterwards).

!!! abstract "At a glance"
    - **Driving question:** Why does a seismometer record two bursts of shaking from one quake,
      and why does the gap between them grow with distance?
    - **Time:** 10 min (whole class, on the projector).
    - **Setup:** Browser only. Two cartoons with no real data, so there are no data services to
      allow: they work wherever this site loads.
    - **Apps and tools:** [Quake Waves](../apps/quake-waves.md) ·
      [How P and S waves move rock](../app/wave-motion/index.html) (also on
      [Seismic waves](../theory/waves.md#two-body-waves))
    - **Before this:** Nothing. This is a first look at P and S waves;
      [Quake Locator](wave-race-card.md) comes next.
    - **Materials:** A projector. Student devices only for the extension.
    - **Utah standards:** PHYS.4.1 · ESS.2.2 ([crosswalk](standards.md#quake-waves))

**Students will:** describe how P and S waves move rock (P along its path, S across it); explain
why P arrives first; predict, then explain, why the gap between P and S grows with distance;
see how the distances from several stations point to one place.

!!! tip "Before class"
    Open both animations on the classroom computer and press **▶ Play** once in each: neither
    starts by itself. Quake Waves starts at **4× faster**, so one run takes about 10 seconds;
    leave it there. Both use Quake Locator's speeds (P 5.8 km/s, S 3.3 km/s). They are cartoons:
    the quake is at the surface, all the rock is the same and the motion is exaggerated.

## 10-minute version (whole class, on the projector)

1. **Hook (1 min).** "A seismometer 50 km from a quake records two bursts of shaking, a few
   seconds apart. Were there two quakes?" Collect a few ideas; don't answer yet.
2. **Predict, then watch the rock (3 min).** Open How P and S waves move rock. Ask: "When a
   wave crosses the rock, does the rock travel with it?" and "One wave squeezes the rock, the
   other shakes it sideways. Which is faster?" Take a show of hands, then press **▶ Play**
   (about 20 seconds). Point at the colored dot in each block: it moves and comes back to its
   spot, so the wave travels on but the rock stays. P squeezes and stretches the rock along its
   path; S shakes it across its path. S falls behind: after 60 km it is about 8 seconds late.
   Rock resists squeezing more than shearing, so P is faster.
3. **Predict, then watch the race (4 min).** Open Quake Waves: a quake (orange star) and four
   stations, A to D. Ask: "Which station feels P first? Which waits longest between P and S?"
   Take a vote, then press **▶ Play**. Station A, 23 km away, waits 3.0 s; station B, 94 km
   away, waits 12.3 s. Why: both waves leave the quake at the same moment, and the faster one
   gains the same lead for every kilometer (about 0.13 s per km), so the longer the race, the
   bigger the gap. Each row turns its S − P into a distance, each distance becomes a circle, and
   the four circles cross at the quake.
4. **Move the quake (2 min).** "If the quake were right next to station C, what would its
   S − P be?" Click the map beside C (it plays again by itself; almost zero: the waves have no distance to
   race). Then click a corner of the map, outside the stations: "Will the circles still cross
   at the quake?" (Yes, in a cartoon, but at shallow angles. With real picks, which are always
   a little off, the crossing smears out:
   [why the stations' positions matter](../theory/locating.md#why-the-stations-positions-matter).)

Close with the rule students take to Quake Locator: the gap between P and S is a distance, about
7.7 km for every second of S − P at these speeds.

## Exit ticket

1. One station records S − P = 5 s, another 10 s. Which is farther from the quake? About how far
   is each, at 7.7 km per second of S − P?
2. Does the rock under a station travel there from the quake? Use what the colored dot did.
3. Why does the gap between P and S grow with distance?
4. P travels 5.8 km/s and S 3.3 km/s. How long does each take to reach a station 58 km away,
   and what is S − P there?

**Answers:** (1) The 10 s station, twice as far: about 38 km and 77 km. (2) No. Each bit of rock
moves back and forth (P) or side to side (S) and returns; the wave carries energy, not rock.
(3) Both waves leave at the same moment and P is faster, so it gains the same lead for every
extra kilometer; the farther the station, the bigger the lead. (4) P 10 s, S about 17.6 s,
S − P about 7.6 s.

## Common difficulties

- **"The second burst was a second quake."** One quake sends out both waves at the same moment;
  they arrive apart because they travel at different speeds.
- **"The rock travels from the quake to us."** The colored dot comes back to its spot. What
  travels is the wave: energy passed from rock to rock.
- **"The gap grows because the waves slow down."** In the cartoon both keep a steady speed; the
  gap grows only because one is faster. (Real rock gets faster with depth:
  [Travel times and Earth's layers](../theory/travel-times.md).)
- **"A bigger gap means a bigger quake."** The gap measures distance, not size.
- **"Real circles cross exactly too."** In the cartoon they do, because the arrival times are
  exact. With real picks they
  [never quite meet](../theory/locating.md#circles-never-meet-perfectly).

## Extension ideas

- **Pairs on their own devices (10–15 min).** Each pair moves the quake to five places and
  records one station's S − P and distance each time. Plot distance against S − P: the points
  fall on a straight line through zero, with a slope of about 7.7 km per second. Compare with
  the formula on [Distance from S minus P](../theory/s-minus-p.md).
- **Speeds from the cartoon.** Drag the slider in How P and S waves move rock and read the time
  (top left) when each pulse reaches the 60 km mark. Speed = distance ÷ time.
- **Next lesson:** [Quake Locator](wave-race-card.md) uses the same rule on real Utah records, where
  picking S is hard and the circles never quite meet.
- **Earth's interior (ESS.2.2):** a liquid does not spring back when it is sheared, so S waves
  cannot cross one. That is how seismologists found that Earth's outer core is liquid
  ([Seismic waves](../theory/waves.md)).
